# L1: wool on spare land (D105, D107)

## Built (in the core, no switch)
- **`systems/pasture.rs`, after cultivation.**
  - A place grazes free land within its cultivation reach, up to `(farmers − arable_labour × fields) / pasture_labour` cells (per km²).
  - It takes the cells of lowest arable rent first, `yield × (1 − τ c_market)`, and gives up its best-rented pasture first when labour falls short.
  - Arable has first call: a grazed cell that cultivation claims stops being pasture. Grazing keeps land clear, as fields do.
- **Wool.**
  - A cell yields `acres × sheep_per_acre × fleece_lb / 364` sacks a year, sold at the outside ports at `wool_price_sack` (£5). That is turned into grain at the wheat price and `bu_per_person`, and valued at `port_world_price`: 25.5 coin a km².
  - The ports pay the full price (`money.wool_in`, coin in).
  - The farm-gate value, less carriage at `market_tau / rho_wool` (ρ 50), splits by `market_share`: the lords' share to the place's market purse, the rest to the farmers less the toll, which goes to the market purse. Carriage goes to the market purse of the place on the port side.
  - Wool rides the seller's paths to the port (`Flow.wool`), so it loads roads. It does not grow W.
- **Labour.** Shepherds are added to the D92 labour guard. `arable_labour` 40 a km² is a calibration from L0's model-implied 39–45.
- **Coin.** The conservation test includes wool and passes. Ports pay a fixed price for wool (no elasticity), a reading.

## Findings
1. **A's labour rule puts pasture where farmers are dense, which is on rich land.**
   - A place can spare shepherds only if its farmers outnumber `arable_labour` × its fields. Its farmers are the people its fields feed, about 75 × yield a km² (D92, the cultivation margin).
   - So only places with fields yielding above about 0.53 have spare labour. Poor-land villages, which history turns to grass, never herd.
   - Within a place's reach the poorest cells go first, but across the region pasture follows the rich, dense places.
2. **Pasture falls after the plague in the core, where L0 had it rise.**
   - After 1348, fields are released only with hysteresis, so farmers per field fall and spare labour goes negative.
   - By 1450 the model's population has recovered (the plague is regained in about 50 years), and the land is wanted for arable again.
   - The gate assumes 1450 is still depopulated, as England was until about 1500. The model's demography recovers faster than the record. L0's rise to 1400 came from its labour measure, the region's farmers per field recomputed each year.
3. **Outside trade (grain and imports) falls to 1%.** Wool's coin lifts grain prices to 0.9–1.05, above the world price of 0.6, so the ports stop buying grain. Wool itself, which is outside trade, is not in the check's definition.
4. **Coin works.**
   - Conservation is exact.
   - Wool brings 44–49% of coin entering before 1348, about the expected half, and 80–87% after.
   - The price level rises but stays in band. Its early marks (years 100–150) sit at 0.31–0.34 because the young region still exports grain at parity.
5. **W with wool** (wool coin to large markets, over grain drawn) is about 0: large markets have no spare labour and are rarely on the port side.

## Second build (D108)
- **Built:**
  - **Grazing rule:** free land in reach not worth ploughing at its distance goes to grass. Its shepherds count in the labour guard.
  - **Later plagues:** Hatcher's outbreaks from 1375 to 1479 enter the Historical calendar, with mortality falling from 0.10 to 0.06 (`plague_later`). The years are the designer's list: I could not check them against Hatcher 1977, whose chronology is not online.
  - **Outside trade** now counts wool, in grain units at the world price, in both the numerator and the gross inflow.
- **One fix made in building:**
  - With no large market in reach, a place sells at home (market cost 0).
  - Before, the cost was infinite, so every free cell in reach read as not worth ploughing. By year 100 that put 591 cells under grass for 6,000 people, with coin at 23 × need.
