## M1 — Map and cost grid

- Map pipeline: heightmap (sea edge, basins, hills, mountain belt) → thermal erosion → stream-power incision (D7) → hydrology (priority-flood fill, D8 flow, drainage area, streams, navigable reaches grown up from the sea, fords, main river mouth) → land cover (yield, natural forest, resources, harbours).
- Maps 1–3: 13,000–14,000 land cells, 160–200 navigable river cells, about 400 fords, 60–95 harbours, mean yield about 0.38.
- Two-layer cost graph: land (Tobler slope, roads, river and stream crossings, fords) and water (sea, river downstream/upstream along flow links), with transshipment at quays, harbours (×1) and open coast (×2), boarding from banks (D10). Bounded Dijkstra with reusable buffers.
- Unit tests on hand-built maps (all pass): flat land costs 1/km and scales with `cell_km`; uphill costs more than downhill; inland to coast takes the river and transships exactly once at 3 + 1 + 0.2/km; upstream costs twice downstream; crossing a river costs 5 except at a ford; harbours transship at half the open-coast cost; Dijkstra respects its cap.
- Viewer: layers panel and travel-cost overlay from a clicked cell. On map 1 the isochrones stretch along the navigable river and the sea beyond the port is one cheap band; heads of navigation and ports show as cost minima.

## M2 — Villages and cultivation

- Cultivation claims free cells in walking-cost order up to `need × cult_margin` within `r_cult`, releases the farthest cells with 10% hysteresis, clears forest at 0.2/yr on fields, regrows 0.02/yr elsewhere, and cuts fuel nearest-first (`fuel_per_capita` 0.00015 cell-units per person per year, up to 0.1 per cell per year).
- Growth as D12.
- Tests (`crates/hinterlands-core/tests/cultivation.rs`, all pass, trade/founding/failure/roads off):
  - a lone village on prime land claims everything within `r_cult` and settles at 150 ± 10 people per cell, with < 1% change over its last 50 years;
  - on yield-0.2 land it reaches under 30% of the prime-land size;
  - fields are cleared; released fields regrow at 2%/yr;
  - beside a navigable river, fields stay on the near bank and stretch along it.
- Map 1, seed 1, food-only, 300 years: 10 villages plateau at about 5,500 each by year 150 (52,260 total), then hold exactly; forest within 5 km of the largest under 2%. Field rings visible around each anchor.

## M3 — Trade and hierarchy

- Trade reworked into the Rihll–Wilson form (D13): marketed share, non-farming population as the attractor, food delivered by gravity with loss.
- Route rendering: per-segment flow summed over paths, width ∝ √flow, brown on land and pale blue on water; fields and roads layers.
- Check, map 1, seed 1, founding/failure/roads off, 60 equal starting villages 5 km apart, β = 0.25, 300 years:
  - a few places pull ahead: the coastal river port (16, 96) at 11,198, then inland market centres at 3,495, 3,276, 3,163 and 3,017, against a village median of about 2,000;
  - the largest share is 0.087 (< 0.25);
  - the leader is on a navigable river and the coast, and is a route hub.
- The rank-size slope over the top 30 is −0.36 in this setup, because 55 of 60 places are land-capped villages of similar size. The slope depends on founding (settlements of different ages) and is checked from M4 on.
- With β = 0.08 one port takes 19% of everything; with β = 0.4 no centre forms (largest 3.8%). The interesting range is 0.15–0.3.

**Open: the main-river-mouth check.** The largest cities form on the coast and on navigable rivers (siting 100%), but at the main mouth in only 30–40% of runs. With β ≈ 0.2–0.3 trade is regional: villages send their marketed grain to markets within a few km, so long river-to-sea trade, which is what historically made mouth cities, barely exists. Making the mouth win needs a long-distance trade component (for example an external sea market, or a second trade tier with weak distance decay) — a shape change beyond v1's scope as written. 3. Footpaths (cheapest walking paths to fields and the two nearest neighbours, where no road runs), parish boundaries (cells given to the settlement that reaches them most cheaply), hearth and charcoal smoke.
4.
